Sociology of race, disability & institutionsOrlando · 2026

I study race, disability, and institutions.

I study how race, disability, and class shape the ways schools and public systems understand children’s needs and distribute support. My current projects use Florida school records, national survey data, and policy documents to examine those decisions, with a broader interest in how they shape Black young people’s experiences and opportunities over time.

A recurring problem

An institutional record can show who received support. To understand the pattern, I also ask how the decision was made and what the record leaves out.

What I keep coming back to

Who gets counted, and as what?

I read school records as the results of decisions made within schools and public systems. The records can show patterns in identification and support; understanding how those patterns emerge also means asking how professional judgment, policy, and unequal conditions shape the decisions.

I want to understand how professional knowledge and institutional rules shape whose needs are recognized, what support they receive, and how those decisions affect young people’s agency and options over time. That question links my current studies of disability identification and service timing to a broader sociology of education, race, disability, and institutions.

When Speech and Language Diverge

Racial and ethnic patterns in Florida speech and language impairment program membership, 2017–2025

Federal reportingSpeech or Language ImpairmentSLI

Florida records Speech Impairment and Language Impairment as separate categories, but federal reporting combines them. Using eight years of records from all 67 Florida school districts, I examine whether combining the categories obscures differences in program membership among Black, Hispanic, and White students.

Intellectual trajectory

From speech-language pathology to sociology

I entered college planning to become a school-based speech-language pathologist. I hoped to work with Black and Latino children from low-resource communities, and studying special education soon raised questions about how schools decide who needs services.

Sociology lets me study speech-language pathology alongside schools, policies, and other institutions that shape how disability is understood and support is distributed.
