I’m an undergraduate sociologist at the University of Central Florida. My research concerns race, disability, and professional expertise, with a focus on speech-language pathology and Black children’s experiences of schools and therapy.

Finding my way to sociology

Kingston→New Jersey→Kingston→Florida

Before college, I worked in service jobs and healthcare, where I helped uninsured patients navigate referrals, medication access, and follow-up care. I saw how insurance, referrals, and medication costs affected whether people could obtain care.

When I entered college, I planned to become a school-based speech-language pathologist working with Black and Latino children from low-resource communities. I wanted to work in schools and help children get speech and language services.

Learning about special education made me question definitions I had initially taken for granted. Who decides what counts as a communication disorder, and whose language provides the standard? Why are some students identified for services while others are overlooked? I became interested in how professional knowledge, school rules, race, and class shape those decisions, which brought me to sociology.

Speech and language remain one important site in my research. My thesis examines racial and ethnic patterns in Florida’s separate Speech Impairment and Language Impairment categories. Other projects examine the timing of developmental services and the detail lost when federal reporting combines state categories. Keeping speech and language categories separate lets me ask whether a combined count conceals differences between them.

Black feminist thought, Critical Race Theory, Critical Disability Studies, and the social model of disability are central to how I think about this work. I use these readings to think about who sets diagnostic standards, how schools treat Black disabled students, and how students can have more say in assessment and services.

I think of myself as a sociologist interested in disability, history, and professional expertise. I want to know how speech-language pathology became an authority on communication and whose language its standards treat as normal. I’m interested in Black children’s experiences of therapy, including how assessment and treatment affect language identity, AAVE, and code-switching. Quantitative methods help me answer some questions. Archives and retrospective accounts could help me answer others.
