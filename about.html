I’m an undergraduate sociologist at the University of Central Florida. My interests connect the history of diagnostic expertise with Black disabled children’s experiences of assessment, therapy, and school.

Finding my way to sociology

Kingston→New Jersey→Kingston→Florida

Before college, I worked in service jobs and healthcare, where I helped uninsured patients navigate referrals, medication access, and follow-up care. I saw how insurance, referrals, and medication costs affected whether people could obtain care.

When I entered college, I planned to become a school-based speech-language pathologist working with Black and Latino children from low-resource communities. I wanted to work in schools and help children get speech and language services.

Studying special education shifted my attention toward the authority behind clinical decisions. I became interested in how a profession establishes the distinction between disorder and language difference, how its standards become accepted in schools, and how racism shapes those judgments. Those questions led me to sociology and remain central to how I think about speech-language pathology.

Speech and language remain one important site in my research. My thesis examines racial and ethnic patterns in Florida’s separate Speech Impairment and Language Impairment categories. Other projects examine the timing of developmental services and the detail lost when federal reporting combines state categories. Keeping speech and language categories separate lets me ask whether a combined count conceals differences between them.

Black feminist theory, Critical Race Theory, and Critical Disability Studies inform how I approach professional knowledge. I’m interested in how white supremacy and anti-Blackness shaped diagnostic standards, whose accounts of communication were accepted as expertise, and how Black disabled people understand the care they receive. The social model of disability also informs my distinction between impairment and the social conditions that restrict access and self-determination.

I see historical sociology and the sociology of professions as ways to investigate how speech-language pathology’s diagnostic authority was established. I’m also interested in Black children’s experiences of therapy and adults’ retrospective accounts of assessment and treatment, including questions about African American English, code-switching, and language identity. My current quantitative work examines program membership and service timing. Historical records and personal accounts would require different methods and answer different parts of this agenda.
