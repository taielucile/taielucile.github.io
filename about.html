Taiel Lucile

I’m an undergraduate sociologist at the University of Central Florida. I was born in Jamaica and grew up between Jamaica and the United States. My route to sociology began with plans to become a speech-language pathologist.

Finding my way to sociology

Kingston→New Jersey→Kingston→Florida

Before college, I worked in service jobs and healthcare. At Brevard Health Alliance, I helped uninsured patients arrange referrals, obtain medication, and follow up after emergency-department visits.

I started college hoping to work with Black and Latino children from low-resource communities as a school-based speech-language pathologist. Speech and language still matter to me, though the questions I ask have changed.

The more I studied special education, the more I found myself asking about the categories themselves. How had clinicians learned to distinguish a disorder from a language difference? Why did schools accept their judgments? I wanted to understand how racism shaped what counted as normal communication. Eventually, I realized I was asking sociological questions.

My thesis compares racial and ethnic patterns in Florida’s Speech Impairment and Language Impairment programs. Florida reports the categories separately; federal reporting combines them. I ask whether that combination hides differences between the two. Other projects examine when children begin developmental services and how state disability categories correspond to federal ones.

Black feminist theory, Critical Race Theory, and Critical Disability Studies are central to my thinking. They have helped me ask how professional expertise can carry racial assumptions and how disabled people’s accounts complicate clinical definitions of their needs. I take seriously both the need for services and the harms people can experience while receiving them.

I’d like to study the profession’s history through its archives. I keep returning, too, to what Black adults remember about childhood therapy. What were they told about their speech, and what were they asked to change? I’m interested in how they made sense of those experiences later, including their relationship to African American English and code-switching.
