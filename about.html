I’m an undergraduate sociologist at the University of Central Florida. I study race and disability, with interests in the history of speech-language pathology and Black people’s experiences of assessment and therapy.

Finding my way to sociology

Kingston→New Jersey→Kingston→Florida

Before college, I worked in service jobs and healthcare. At Brevard Health Alliance, I helped uninsured patients arrange referrals, obtain medication, and follow up after emergency-department visits.

I entered college planning to become a school-based speech-language pathologist working with Black and Latino children from low-resource communities.

As I studied special education, I became less willing to take its categories for granted. I wanted to understand how clinicians learned to distinguish disorder from language difference and why their judgments carried authority in schools. I also wanted to know how racism shaped what counted as normal communication. Those questions brought me to sociology.

My thesis compares racial and ethnic patterns in Florida’s Speech Impairment and Language Impairment programs. Florida reports the categories separately; federal reporting combines them. I ask whether that combination hides differences between the two. Other projects examine when children begin developmental services and how state disability categories correspond to federal ones.

Black feminist theory, Critical Race Theory, and Critical Disability Studies are central to my thinking. They have helped me ask how professional expertise can carry racial assumptions and how disabled people’s accounts complicate clinical definitions of their needs. I take seriously both the need for services and the harms people can experience while receiving them.

I want to study the history of speech-language pathology through professional archives. I’m also interested in Black adults’ memories of childhood therapy: what they were told about their speech, what they were asked to change, and how they understood those experiences later. These questions include African American English, code-switching, and the relationship between therapy and language identity.
