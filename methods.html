Methods

How I work

My methods follow the question and the evidence available. In my current work, I use administrative records, survey data, and policy documents to study how institutions define need and distribute support. I pay attention to how data are produced, who they include, and how analytical choices shape a result. Quantitative analysis is one part of my work; I want to develop historical and community-engaged approaches for questions the existing records leave open.

Before the model

Understanding the records

A variable such as program membership reflects a reporting process as well as a student’s circumstances. I read the documentation to understand that process, including changes in eligibility rules, category definitions, and the treatment of small counts.

  • What exactly is being counted?
  • Who is missing?
  • Did the definition change?
  • What was suppressed or combined?
  • What can I actually say from this?

Methods I use now

Complex surveys

National Survey of Children’s Health

For Who Gets Help First?, I combine two years of survey responses and account for the sampling design when estimating differences between groups. I also use the survey’s multiply imputed poverty measures so that missing income information is handled within the analysis. Regression models and adjusted probabilities help me compare service timing while accounting for the other characteristics included in the models.

Administrative records

Florida special-education data

The Florida study required combining 16 workbooks into a consistent dataset, then checking that district, year, and racial-group records matched correctly. I calculate program-membership rates and risk ratios and examine how they change under alternative enrollment denominators, district-size cutoffs, and assumptions about suppressed cells. These checks help distinguish a stable pattern from one that depends heavily on a data choice.

Policy evaluation

Medicaid expansion and special education

In The Staying Power of Special Education, I use state-by-year data and a staggered difference-in-differences design to examine changes in IDEA identification following Medicaid expansion. The design uses differences in policy timing across states; its interpretation depends on the assumptions required for those comparisons.

Policy research

Speech and language reporting

For the federal reporting project, I compare the categories named in state documents with those used in federal reporting. I retain the original definitions and track the source for each comparison. If the documents do not explain how two categories correspond, I mark the relationship as uncertain rather than assume they mean the same thing.

Reproducibility

Keeping the analysis traceable

I keep the source files and write the cleaning and analysis steps in code, so I can follow a result back to the records and decisions that produced it. Git lets me track changes as the work develops. Checks for duplicate records, incorrect merges, impossible values, and missing data are part of that process, along with rerunning analyses under reasonable alternative definitions.

What I want to learn next

Methods for questions I cannot answer yet

I want to connect patterns in institutional records to how disabled young people experience and remember the systems those records describe. I plan to extend my training in longitudinal analysis and develop historical and community-engaged approaches to questions about institutional change and young people’s lives. The approaches below describe where I want to develop, rather than methods I already claim to practice.

Quantitative

Trajectories and institutional conditions

I want to learn how longitudinal and multilevel models, event-history and sequence analysis, and record linkage can help me study changes in young people’s lives. Further training in causal inference would strengthen my policy research.

Historical

How institutions came to be

Archival research and historical sociology would let me investigate how professional definitions of disability and school policies developed, including whose knowledge shaped them.

Community-engaged

Experiences institutions do not record

I want to learn how to develop research with disabled young people and their communities, including interviews and other approaches that make room for their accounts of school and service systems.

Critical and theoretical

How I frame the problem

I’m continuing to read Black feminist thought, Critical Race Theory, Critical Disability Studies, DisCrit, and Black disability scholarship. These traditions inform how I ask questions about normality, authority, racism, and ableism.
