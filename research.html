Research

Race, disability, and institutional life

I’m a sociologist studying how race, disability, and class shape the ways schools and other institutions understand children’s needs and respond to them. My current projects examine special-education categories in Florida, the timing of developmental services, and the detail lost when federal reporting combines categories. Together, they open onto broader questions about professional knowledge, institutional decisions, and how support is distributed across young people’s lives.

Questions I want to keep asking

My current studies begin with present-day institutional decisions and lead me to broader questions about disability, power, and young people’s experiences in schools.

01How have professions and institutions defined disability, communication, and need, and whose knowledge shaped those definitions?

02How do race, class, language, and disability shape how schools recognize students’ needs and decide where support goes?

03How do institutional decisions shape young people’s experiences at school and their paths into college, work, and adulthood?

04How might research and policy change when disabled young people, especially Black young people, help shape the questions and decisions that affect them?

What I’m readingBlack feminist thought, Critical Race Theory, Critical Disability Studies, DisCrit, and Black disability scholarship help me ask how race, disability, and professional authority fit together. The social model of disability sharpens my attention to the barriers institutions create and the conditions that support young people’s access and agency.

Honors Undergraduate Thesis · University of Central Florida

When Speech and Language Diverge

Racial and Ethnic Patterns in Florida Speech and Language Impairment Program Membership, 2017–2025

01Question 05Limits

Data

The dataset draws on Florida Department of Education workbooks for all 67 county school districts, covering the eight school years from 2017–18 through 2024–25.

Key study decisions

Keep SI and LI separate

Florida reports Speech Impairment and Language Impairment separately, so I preserve that distinction instead of starting with the combined federal category.

Define the denominator

The primary rates use same-race PK–12 enrollment. I rerun the analysis with K–12 enrollment to see whether that choice changes the pattern.

Handle small and suppressed cells

I test alternative district-size cutoffs and bounds for suppressed enrollment cells rather than silently treating suppressed values as zero.

Methods

I calculate program-membership rates and risk ratios in R, compare patterns across districts and years, and check how alternative data choices affect the results.

Question

Do Black, Hispanic, and White students show different patterns of membership in Speech Impairment and Language Impairment programs? Florida’s separate records let me investigate differences that would be harder to see in the combined federal category.

Design

I assembled district-level records for eight school years and calculated program-membership rates for each racial and ethnic group. I compare rates for Black and Hispanic students with those for White students, examine variation across districts and years, and test how the results change when I use different enrollment denominators or assumptions about suppressed counts.

Data
Florida Department of Education administrative workbooks

Scale
16 workbooks · 1,608 district-year-race rows

Methods
R · program-membership rates · risk ratios · district and year comparisons · sensitivity checks

Status
Analysis complete; manuscript in progress

What these data cannot tell me: these records count students enrolled in programs under the school system’s reporting rules. They cannot establish the prevalence of speech or language disorders, whether a particular student was correctly identified, or whether discrimination explains a difference between groups. They also do not describe the services students received or their experiences of identification. Those limits matter when interpreting the patterns.

Independent research

Who Gets Help First?

Racial and Socioeconomic Differences in the Timing of Developmental Service Entry Among U.S. Children

Study at a glance

Data
2023–2024 National Survey of Children’s Health
Sample
Children who received developmental services
Analysis
Survey weights · regression · adjusted probabilities
Missing data
Multiply imputed household-poverty measures

Receiving developmental services can happen at very different points in childhood. This study asks how the timing differs by race, ethnicity, and household poverty among children who have received services. I pool the 2023 and 2024 National Survey of Children’s Health, account for its sampling design and imputed poverty measures, and compare adjusted probabilities across groups. Because the sample includes service recipients, the study addresses timing within that group rather than access among all children who might need support.

What Federal Special-Education Data Hide

A Policy Case for Disaggregating Speech and Language Impairment

State reporting

Speech Impairment+Language Impairment

Federal reporting

Speech or Language ImpairmentOne combined category
The distinction disappears.

When two state categories are reported as one federal category, the public federal data cannot show whether patterns differ between Speech Impairment and Language Impairment.

The Florida study raised a second question: how much detail disappears when states report their data to the federal government? I’m comparing state and federal documents to understand which speech and language categories states use and how those categories are combined. This companion paper examines the consequences for research and reporting, including cases where the documentation does not explain the relationship clearly enough to draw a firm conclusion.
